1. Artificial Neural Network

Let's imagine that our body receives a signal, say through the eyes. This signal goes through the eyes and flows from one group of neurons to the next until it reaches a certain place where we make a certain judgement about the signal, say whether it is a cat or a flower. How to make a mathematical model of this process?

Let $x$ be a signal. After receiving the signal, the input signal $x$ goes to a first group neurons $N_{1}^1,N_{2}^1,..,N_{k_1}^1$, neurons in the first layer, each $N_{i}^1$ processes information it received and passes it to deeper group of neuron $N_{1}^2,...,N_{k_2}^2$, neurons in the second layer, this process continues until the signal reaches a certain depth $L$ with neurons $N_{1}^L,...,N_{k_{L}}^L$, neurons in the L-th layer, and the result from these last group combined to produce an output.

The simplest way to model a neuron $N_{j}^i$ processing its inputs is to add all the inputs it receives to get a single number. However, a neuron has different connection with other neuron, so instead of doing $\sum x_{\kc{k}}^{i-1}$, we do weighted sum $\sum w_{\kc{k},\jc{j}}^{i}x_{\kc{k}}^{i-1}$. So here $w_{\kc{k},\jc{j}}^{i}$ captures the strength of connection of neuron $N_{\kc{k}}^{i-1}$ at layer $i-1$ with neuron $N_{\jc{j}}^{i}$ at layer $i$. The weighted sum can be seen as dot product between the weighted vector $W_{\jc{j}}^{i}=(w_{\kc{1},\jc{j}}^{i},w_{\kc{2},\jc{j}}^{i},\ldots,w_{\kc{k_{i-1}},\jc{j}}^{i})^T$ and the output of layer $i-1$ vector $X^{i-1}=(x_{\kc{1}}^{i-1},\ldots,x_{\kc{k_{i-1}}}^{i-1})^T$, we have $x_{\jc{j}}^{\jc{i}}=(W_{\jc{j}}^{\jc{i}})^TX^{\kc{i-1}}$.

\(w_{\kc{1},\jc{j}}^{i}\)\(w_{\kc{2},\jc{j}}^{i}\)\(w_{\kc{k_{i-1}},\jc{j}}^{i}\)Neuron \(N_{\kc{1}}^{i-1}\)Neuron \(N_{\kc{2}}^{i-1}\)Neuron \(N_{\kc{k_{i-1}}}^{i-1}\)Neuron \(N_{\jc{j}}^{i}\)layer \(i-1\)layer \(i\)
\(x_{\kc{1}}^{i-1}\)\(x_{\kc{2}}^{i-1}\)\(x_{\kc{k_{i-1}}}^{i-1}\)\(X^{i-1}=\)\(w_{\kc{1},\jc{j}}^{i}\)\(w_{\kc{2},\jc{j}}^{i}\)\(w_{\kc{k_{i-1}},\jc{j}}^{i}\)\(W_{\jc{j}}^{i}=\)\(x_{\jc{j}}^{i}\)layer \(i-1\)layer \(i\)

The weight vector $W_{\jc{j}}^{i}$ represents the strength of connection neuron $N_{\jc{j}}^{i}$ has to those in layer $i-1$. The total output of layer $i-1$ is packed into a vector $X^{i-1}$. For other neurons in layer $i$, they have their weight vector $W_{\jc{m}}^{i}$, and the signal it gets from layer $i-1$ is also represented as a dot product of $X^{i-1}$ with the weight $W^{i-1}_{\jc{m}}$. So the output of layer $i$ is a vector

\[X^{i}=\begin{bmatrix} (W_{\jc{1}}^{i})^TX^{i-1}\\ \vdots \\ (W_{\jc{k_i}}^{i})^TX^{i-1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} (W_{\jc{1}}^{i})^T\\ \vdots \\ (W_{\jc{k_i}}^{i})^T\end{bmatrix}X^{i-1}=\begin{bmatrix} w_{\kc{1},\jc{1}}^{i} & w_{\kc{2},\jc{1}}^{i} & \cdots & w_{\kc{k_{i-1}},\jc{1}}^{i}\\ w_{\kc{1},\jc{2}}^{i} & w_{\kc{2},\jc{2}}^{i} & \cdots & w_{\kc{k_{i-1}},\jc{2}}^{i}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ w_{\kc{1},\jc{k_i}}^{i} & w_{\kc{2},\jc{k_i}}^{i} & \cdots & w_{\kc{k_{i-1}},\jc{k_i}}^{i}\end{bmatrix}X^{i-1}\]

We represent the collection of weighted vectors as the matrix $W^{i}=[W_{\jc{1}}^{i},\ldots,W_{\jc{k_i}}^{i}]^T$, so $X^i=W^{i}X^{i-1}$. So we see that starting from input, which is represented as a vector $X^0$, the neurons in the first layer produce $X^1=W^1X^0$, and this input gets fed to the neurons in the second layer to produce $X^2=W^2X^1$ and so on. We obtain a dynamical system \[\begin{cases} X^0=\text{input}\\ X^{i+1}=W^{i+1}X^i & \text{output of neuron layer } i+1\end{cases}\]

\(X^0\)
\(X^1=W^1X^0\)
\(X^2=W^2X^1\)
\(\cdots\)
\(X^L=W^{L}X^{L-1}\)

Now there is an issue, \[X^L=W^{L}X^{L-1}=W^{L}(W^{L-1}X^{L-2})=\cdots=(W^{L}\cdots W^{1})X^0,\] but the product of matrices $W^{L},W^{L-1},\ldots,W^{1}$ gives another matrix, call it $W$, i.e., $X^L=WX^0$. What this means is we can think of the whole process as just one layer from $X^0$ to $X^L$ with one matrix multiplication.

To give a sense of multi-layers, we need to go back to examine how information is transmitted in between two neurons. Let's say we have neuron A transmit information to neuron B. A doesn't continuously send information to B, there is a gap in their synaptic connection.

A only sends information to B if it has enough information. What this means is that there is a gate between the synaptic connection and this gate opens when certain condition is met. This phenomenon happens in other places in nature too. For example, we have a ceiling that water seeps in. The water doesn't continuously drip down because there is surface tension, so it accumulates until gravity has enough strength to pull it down.

\(b\)\(x\)\(\text{ReLu}(x-b)\)

To model this gate phenomenon, we use a function called rectified linear function $\text{ReLu}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ defined by $\text{ReLu}(x)=x$ if $x$ is positive and $\text{ReLu}(x)=0$ if $x\le 0$. This function acts as a gate: it only allows information to pass forward if it is greater than 0. Now we want to allow a neuron to have its own threshold other than 0. So for neuron $N_{\jc{j}}^{i}$, it has a threshold $b_{\jc{j}}^{i}$. And the gate function is $\text{ReLu}(x-b_{\jc{j}}^{i})$. This mechanism is called self gate.

To apply this gating process to a layer of neurons, we use bias vector $b^i=(b_{\jc{1}}^i,\ldots,b_{\jc{k_i}}^i)^T$ and the dynamical equation above is upgraded to \[\begin{cases} X^0=\text{input}\\ X^{i+1}=\text{ReLu}(W^{i+1}X^i+b^{i+1})\end{cases}\] where the ReLu is applied component wise to the vector $W^{i+1}X^i+b^{i+1}$.

The gate function is often called activation function since we can think the neuron gets activated when the threshold is reached. In the $n$ dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^n$, the map from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$ given by $L(x)=Wx$ with $W$ an $m$ by $n$ matrix is called a linear map. The collection of these maps is denoted by $\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^n,\mathbb{R}^m)$, and this can be shown to be equal to the set of $m$ by $n$ matrices $M_{m\times n}$. The map $A(x)=Wx+b$ is called affine map, it generalises the line equation $y=ax+b$ in one dimension. The gate/activation function is usually denoted by $\sigma$.

Definition

A neural network is a function obtained by composing affine linear functions with non-linear functions. More precisely, a neural network of $ L $ layers is a function $ NN: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m $ defined by $ NN(x) = Af_L \circ \sigma_{L-1} \circ \cdots \circ Af_2 \circ \sigma_1 \circ Af_1(x) $ where each $ Af_i: \mathbb{R}^{k_{i-1}} \to \mathbb{R}^{k_i} $ is an affine map, i.e., $ Af_i(x) = W^{i} x + b^i $, with $ W^{i}\in \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{k_{i-1}},\mathbb{R}^{k_i})$ and $b^i \in \mathbb{R}^{k_i} $ and $k_0=n$ and $k_L=m$. Each $ \sigma_i $ is a non-linear function applied pointwise to the output of $Af_i $.

Let me clarify what we mean by "applied pointwise" here. Say we have a function $f(x):\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}$, and $v=(v_1,v_2,..,v_N)$ is a vector. Applying $f$ on $v$ pointwise is $f(v)=(f(v_1),f(v_2),...,f(v_N))$.

The model without gate treats the flow of information as continuous, while the one with gate takes into account that information can stop propagating if it doesn't have enough energy.

The picture we should have in mind of a neural network is a collection of neurons ordered as layers, and between them there are connections, and each neuron has a threshold associated to it, usually known as bias. The vector $b^i$ is called bias vector. The strength of connection between neurons is kept in the matrix $W^i$. Below is the picture we should have in mind where the strength of connection is labeled with color from blue to red.

\(-1\)\(1\)

The structure of the neural network, i.e., how many layers there are and how many neurons in each layer, together with the choice of the activation function is called the architecture of the neural network.

The $\text{ReLu}(x-b)$ function with threshold $b$ decides if $x>b$, it will send the input forward, and if not, it doesn't. There is no middle ground. This is known as hard gate. To get a soft gate, people use $\text{SiLu}(x-b)=(x-b)\,\text{sigmoid}(x-b)$, where $\text{sigmoid}(x)=e^{x}/(1+e^{x})$ is a number between 0 and 1, so the input is let through partly. Below is the graph of the hard gate and our new soft gate.

\(b\)\(x\)
hard gate \(\text{ReLu}(x-b)\) soft gate \(\text{SiLu}(x-b)\)

The reason we call it soft gate is that when $x$ is large, $\text{sigmoid}(x)$ is approximately 1, so $\text{SiLu}(x)\sim x\times 1=x$. And when $x$ is very small (in negative sense) $\text{sigmoid}(x)\sim 0$, so $\text{SiLu}(x)\sim 0$. However, when $x$ is close to 0, the $\text{SiLu}(x)$ is not just 0 as we see in $\text{ReLu}(x)$.

People also use sigmoid function, tanh, and others as activation function. \[\text{sigmoid}(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}},\qquad \tanh(x)=\frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{e^{x}+e^{-x}},\qquad \text{softplus}(x)=\ln(1+e^{x})\]

2. Digression: Metric Spaces

Distance is a quantitative method that allows us to say how close or far two things are to each other. In space $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$, we have distance formula given by Pythagorean theorem

\[\text{Distance from } A \text{ to } B=\sqrt{(a_1-b_1)^2+(a_2-b_2)^2}\]

\(A=(a_1,a_2)\)\(B=(b_1,b_2)\)\(|b_1-a_1|\)\(|b_2-a_2|\)distance\(a_1\)\(a_2\)

This formula also has an analogue in higher dimension, where now we have points $A=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ and $B=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and

\[\text{Distance from } A \text{ to } B=\sqrt{(a_1-b_1)^2+(a_2-b_2)^2+\cdots+(a_n-b_n)^2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_i-b_i)^2}\]

\(\color[RGB]{207,90,78}{d(x,y)}\le\color[RGB]{62,156,139}{d(x,z)+d(z,y)}\)

In mathematics, distance is not only restricted to locations, but it is between two objects. For example, we have distance between two numbers $a$ and $b$ defined as $|a-b|$. It is very important to be able to say when two things are similar or close to each other in an objective manner. To do this, we need to talk about metric space. What this means is that we can find a way to measure distance between objects of interest even if they are not locations as we conventionally think about. Let us denote the collection of objects we study as $X$. Our goal is to find a way to measure distance between two points $x_1$ and $x_2$ of $X$. This distance is denoted by $d(x_1,x_2)$. We say $d$ is a metric if it satisfies the following conditions

  1. $d(x,y)\geq 0$, and $d(x,y)=0$ if and only if $x=y$.
  2. $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$.
  3. $d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)+d(z,y)$.

The first condition just means that if $x$ and $y$ are different points, their distance must be greater than 0, and distance between $x$ and $x$ itself is 0. The second condition says distance is symmetric, meaning distance from $x$ to $y$ is the same as from $y$ to $x$. The third is known as triangle inequality and it means that if you travel from $x$ to $y$ by detouring to $z$ first, then you increase your traveling path.

The Euclidean distance that we talk about satisfies these three conditions. First note that distance

\[d(A,B)=\sqrt{(a_1-b_1)^2+(a_2-b_2)^2}.\]

Proof of 1 and 2. Both terms $(a_1-b_1)^2$ and $(a_2-b_2)^2$ are $\geq 0$, so the sum is $\geq 0$ and so is its square root. If $d(A,B)=0$, then a sum of two terms that are $\geq 0$ equals 0, so both terms are 0, that is $a_1=b_1$ and $a_2=b_2$, and $A=B$. If $A=B$, both terms are 0 and $d(A,B)=0$. This is condition 1. For condition 2, $(a_1-b_1)^2=(b_1-a_1)^2$ and $(a_2-b_2)^2=(b_2-a_2)^2$, so $d(A,B)=d(B,A)$.

The proof of the triangle inequality of property 3 is a bit more involved and we prefer not to give it here.

\(A\)\(B\)\(d_2(A,B)=5\)\(d_1(A,B)=4+3=7\)

Now I want to point to the fact that there is more than one way to measure distance. Imagine you try to build an app similar to Grab. If you measure distance using a straight line as the Euclidean distance does, the driver would not be happy because that is not how they drive their tuk-tuk. They drive their tuk-tuk based on the grid streets of the city. So it makes more sense to measure distance that way. This gives rise to what is known as taxicab metric, denoted by

\[d_1(A,B)=|a_1-b_1|+|a_2-b_2|.\]

To give a notation for the Euclidean metric, we denote it by $d_2$.

Beside the metrics $d_1$ and $d_2$, we have a more general notion of metric denoted by

\[d_p(A,B)=\left(|a_1-b_1|^p+|a_2-b_2|^p\right)^{1/p}\]

for any $p\geq 1$. These metrics satisfy all the desired properties 1, 2, 3 we mentioned earlier.

In this course, our object of interest is function. Consider a function $f:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}$. And we have another function $g:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}$. We want to have a way to say they are close to each other. People have come up with several ways of doing this. One approach is they look at the gap between the two functions at each point, and the largest gap is called the distance. In particular they define

\[d(f,g)=\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|f(x)-g(x)|.\]

\(-1\)\(1\)\(x\)\(f\)\(g\)\(d(f,g)\)

The collection of continuous functions from $[-1,1]$ to $\mathbb{R}$ is denoted by $C[-1,1]$. Together with distance $d_\infty(f,g)=\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|f(x)-g(x)|$, $C[-1,1]$ is a metric space. What this means is that we can study when two functions are close to each other or not. This is important to us because we want to approximate a function we need, so we must have some way to tell us when two functions are close or not. More generally, if $X$ is a nice subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$, we can study continuous functions from $X$ to $\mathbb{R}$, or $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the collection of these functions, denoted by $C(X)$, forms a metric space with the metric

\[d_\infty(f,g)=\max_{x\in X}\|f(x)-g(x)\|.\]

Another metric on $C[-1,1]$ is the density of area bounded by the two curves. In particular, given $f$ and $g$ in $C[-1,1]$, we define

\[d_1(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}|f(x)-g(x)|\,dx.\]

\(-1\)\(1\)\(x\)\(f\)\(g\)\(d_1(f,g)=\tfrac12\times\text{shaded area}\)

More generally, for $f$ and $g$ in $C(X)$, we define

\[d_1(f,g)=\frac{1}{\text{area}(X)}\int_{X}\|f(x)-g(x)\|\,dx.\]

One important notion in metric space is density. A subset $A$ of $X$ is called dense in $X$ if we can approximate any element of $X$ by members of $A$, i.e., for any $x$ in $X$ and any $\varepsilon>0$, we can find $a$ in $A$ such that $d(x,a)<\varepsilon$. We see an example of this with the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ being dense in $\mathbb{R}$. We know a great deal about the rational numbers and we can even represent them in the computer. However, we don't know most elements of $\mathbb{R}$ and we can't represent them in any computer. The famous example is $\pi$, the ratio of the circumference and the diameter. This is a very important number, people have been studying it since ancient times, and it appears almost everywhere in science and engineering. To have access to it, we use its rational approximation 3.14, or a longer decimal if we need to be more accurate, and we can make it as accurate as we like since $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$.

Like $\mathbb{R}$, we don't know most functions in $C(X)$. Our goal is to find a suitable candidate like $\mathbb{Q}$ to approximate elements in $C(X)$. By this time, you should realise that anything in the world can be represented as a function, for example the future is the output of the present, therefore if we can approximate any function, we can even predict the future. In the next section, we will see that the set $\mathcal{N}$ of neural networks can approximate any function in $C(X)$ if $X$ is a cube. This means that $\mathcal{N}$ is dense in $C(X)$.

3. Universal Approximation Theorems

A neural network is defined by its weight matrices $W^i$ and bias vectors $b^i$, collectively known as parameters and denoted by $P = (W^1, W^2, \ldots, W^L, b^1, \ldots, b^L)$. These parameters can be thought long vector of dimension $K$ for some $K \in \mathbb{N}$, representing a point $P$ in $\mathbb{R}^K$. Each point corresponds uniquely to a neural network instance, denoted $NN_P$, which is structured according to the network's fixed architecture. This parameter-to-network mapping can be expressed as: $$ P \mapsto NN_P $$ The primary task of machine learning is to select the parameter $P$ that make $NN_P$ best approximates the function of interest. This point of view allow us to see ML is a technique of function approximation much like that of Fourier series and Taylor expansion.

\(\mathbb{R}^2\)\(P=(a_1,a_2)\)\(P\mapsto \text{ReLu}(a_1x+a_2)\)\(C([-1,1])\)\(\text{ReLu}(a_1x+a_2)\)\(-1\)\(1\)

One obvious question is what kind of function can be approximated by neural networks. The answer of this question coming from universal approximation theorems which we are now turn to. First, we look at a theorem due to G. Cybenko in his paper Approximation by Superpositions of a Sigmoidal Function

Universal Approximation Theorem, G. Cybenko: The space $ \mathcal{N}_2 = \bigcup_{N=1}^{\infty} \left\{ \sum_{i}^N\alpha_i \sigma(y_i^T x + b_i) \mid y_i \in \mathbb{R}^d \right\} $ is dense in $ \mathcal{C}(I_d, \mathbb{R}) $, where $ \sigma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} $ is a fixed discriminatory function, to be defined below, and $I_d$ is the unit cube in dimension $d$.

Here dense is in the sense of Section 2 with the metric $d_\infty$. For any $f$ in $\mathcal{C}(I_d,\mathbb{R})$ and any $\varepsilon>0$, we can find $g$ in $\mathcal{N}_2$ such that $d_\infty(f,g)<\varepsilon$, that is $|f(x)-g(x)|<\varepsilon$ for every $x$ in $I_d$.

Definition We say that $\sigma$ is discriminatory if for a measure $\mu \in M(I_n)$, $$ \int_{I_n} \sigma(y^T x + \theta) \, d\mu(x) = 0 $$ for all $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ implies that $\mu = 0$.

Proof

Note that $ \mathcal{N}_2 = \bigcup_{N=1}^{\infty} \left\{ \sum_{i}^N\alpha_i \sigma(y_i^T x + b_i) \mid y_i \in \mathbb{R}^d \right\} $ is a linear subspace of $\mathcal{C}(I_d,\mathbb{R})$. Assume it is not dense, then its closure $\overline{\mathcal{N}_2}$ is a proper Banach subspace of $\mathcal{C}$. We can define a linear functional $\ell:\overline{\mathcal{N}_2}\to \mathbb{R}$ to be zero. Since $\overline{\mathcal{N}_2}$ is a proper subspace, the Hahn-Banach's theorem allows us to extend $\ell$ to all of $\mathcal{C}$ such that $\ell(f)=0$ if $f\in \overline{\mathcal{N}_2}$ and $\ell(f)\neq 0$ for some $f\notin \overline{\mathcal{N}_2}$. In particular, there exist $f_0\in \mathcal{C}$ such that $\ell(f_0)\neq 0$.

Now follow Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem, we know there exist a signed Borel's measure $\mu$ on $I_d$ such that $$ \ell(f)=\int fd\mu $$ for all $f\in \mathcal{C}$. In particular $\int \sigma(y_i^Tx+b)d\mu=0$ for all $y\in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $b\in\mathbb{R}$. But $\sigma$ is discriminatory, this implies $\mu=0$. However, we know that $\int f_0d\mu=\ell(f_0)\neq 0$, this is a contradiction.

The next stage of the proof is to prove that the activation functions we have above are discriminatory with respect to the Lebesgue measure. This is true but we won't be proving it here.

We can equip the space $\mathcal{C}(I_d,\mathbb{R})$ with the $L^p$ norm, and there are similar results by Kurt Hornik in 1990 in his paper Approximation capabilities of multilayer feedforward networks.

Theorem, K. Hornik: The space $ \mathcal{N}_2 = \bigcup_{N=1}^{\infty} \left\{ \sum_{i}^N\alpha_i \sigma(y_i^T x + b_i) \mid y_i \in \mathbb{R}^d \right\} $ is dense in the space of $\mathcal{C}(I_d,\mathbb{R})$ when $\mathcal{C}$ is equipped with the $L^p$ norm with $1\leq p\leq \infty$ and $\sigma$ is a continuous, bounded and non-constant function.

The $L^p$ distance is $d_p(f,g)=\left(\frac{1}{\text{area}(I_d)}\int_{I_d}|f(x)-g(x)|^p\,dx\right)^{1/p}$, and $\text{area}(I_d)=1$. For $p=1$ this is the metric $d_1$ of Section 2. So Cybenko's theorem is density in $d_\infty$ and Hornik's theorem is density in $d_p$.

3.1 Implementation

Let's consider the task of finding a two-layer neural network to approximate $\cos(x)$ for $x \in [-10,10]$ with respect to the $L^2$ norm. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, the Universal Approximation Theorem tells us that there exists a neural network which is $\varepsilon$-close to $\cos(x)$ on this interval in the $L^2$ norm. The question then is which neural network? Unfortunately, there is no straightforward way to identify it directly. What we can do is fix an architecture for the network and then search for the parameters $P$ that work best. We start with a random initial guess for $P$ and use gradient descent to iteratively improve this guess.

Let's set this mathematically. As an example, first, we select the architecture by setting the first layer to have dimension $13$ and the second layer to have dimension $5$.

\(\mathbb{R}^{1}\)\(\mathbb{R}^{13}\)\(\mathbb{R}^{5}\)\(\mathbb{R}^{1}\)\(W^{1}\)\(W^{2}\)\(W^{3}\)

This means we are looking for a function $$ NN_P(x) = W^3(\sigma(W^2\sigma(W^1x+b^1)+b^2))+b^3 $$ where $W^1$ is a matrix that takes $\mathbb{R}^1$ to $\mathbb{R}^{13}$, $W^2$ is a matrix taking $\mathbb{R}^{13}$ to $\mathbb{R}^5$, $W^3$ is a matrix that takes $\mathbb{R}^5$ back to $\mathbb{R}$, and $b^1 \in \mathbb{R}^{13}, b^2 \in \mathbb{R}^5$ and $b^3 \in \mathbb{R}$. In this case, $P = (W^1, W^2, W^3, b^1, b^2, b^3)$ is a point in $\mathbb{R}^{13 \cdot 1 + 13 \cdot 5 + 5 \cdot 1 + 13 + 5 + 1=102}$.

Given $P \in \mathbb{R}^{102}$, we construct a function $NN_P(x)$ to approximate the cosine function. The error of this approximation is measured by the loss function, defined as: $$ \text{loss}(P) = \int_{-10}^{10} (\cos(x) - NN_P(x))^2 \, dx $$ Note that $\text{loss}(P)=20\,d_2(\cos,NN_P)^2$, where $d_2(f,g)=\left(\frac{1}{20}\int_{-10}^{10}|f(x)-g(x)|^2dx\right)^{1/2}$ is the $L^2$ distance on $[-10,10]$. So making the loss small means making $NN_P$ close to $\cos$ in this metric. The task is to optimize $\text{loss}: \mathbb{R}^{102} \to \mathbb{R}$. Of course we can't hope to find $P$ that truly is the minimum since the dimension is so large. What we can hope for is to find $P$ such that $\text{loss}(P)<\varepsilon$ for an acceptable $\varepsilon>0$.

Next, we need to be more realistic. We cannot compute $\text{loss}(P)$ directly because the function $NN_P(x)$ is difficult, if not impossible, to integrate analytically. To address this issue, we define a loss function at each point as: $$ l(P,x) = |\cos(x) - NN_P(x)|^2 $$ and then approximate the overall loss function $\text{loss}(P)$ by: $$ \text{loss}(P) \approx \text{loss}_D = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N l(P,x_i)}{N} $$ where $D = \{x_1, \ldots, x_N\}$ is a set of points sampled from $[-10, 10]$. Here, the subscript $D$ indicates a loss function depends on the data set $D$. Since this loss function is based on the data, it is called the empirical loss. Note that as $N \to \infty$, by continuity, $\text{loss}_D(P)\to \frac{1}{20}\text{loss}(P) = d_2(\cos,NN_P)^2$.

Let's summarize where we are now. We have a dataset $D$. For each data point $x_i \in [-10,10]$, we can evaluate the point-wise loss for each parameter set $P$. Averaging these, we obtain the empirical loss: $$ \text{loss}_D(P) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N |\cos(x_i) - NN_P(x_i)|^2}{N} $$ While this empirical loss is computable, it remains challenging to optimize using critical points method. A method commonly used for this optimization is called gradient descent.

4. Gradient Descent Algorithm

Let's remind ourselves what gradient descent is. Consider $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$. The goal is to find a point $P$ where $f(P)$ is the global minimum of $f$. The gradient descent algorithm suggests the following steps:

  1. Start at a point $P_0$.
  2. Compute the gradient $\nabla f(P_0)$.
  3. Take a small step $\eta>0$ in the direction opposite to the gradient, i.e., $P_1 = P_0 - \eta \nabla f(P_0)$.
  4. Repeat the above process.

The number $\eta$ is known as the learning rate. The reason we move in the direction opposite to the gradient is that the function decreases fastest in this direction.

We now state and prove that if $f$ is a nice function, the gradient descent algorithm will converge to a minimum. Note that most function we encounter in machine learning are far from being nice.

Theorem Let $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}$ be a strongly convex, bounded below, non-constant differentiable function where the derivative $Df:\mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}^d$ of $f$ is Lipschitz. Then $f$ has a unique minimum, we denoted it by $P_c$. Moreover, for $\eta$ sufficiently small, the sequence $$ P_{n+1}=P_n-\eta \nabla f(P_n) $$ converges to $P_c$ for any initial point $P_0$.

Definition Let $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}$. We say $f$ is convex if for all $P,Q\in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$ we have $$ f(\lambda P+(1-\lambda) Q)\leq \lambda f(P)+(1-\lambda)f(Q), $$ which implies $$f(P)-f(Q)\geq \nabla f(Q)^T(P-Q)$$ if $f$ is differentiable. A function $f$ is strongly convex if there exist $\mu>0$ such that $$ f(P)\geq f(Q)+\nabla f(Q)^T(P-Q)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|P-Q\|^2. $$ And $F:\mathbb{R}^d\to \mathbb{R}^d$ is Lipschitz if there exist $L>0$ such that $$ \|F(P)-F(Q)\|\leq L\|P-Q\| $$ for any $P,Q\in \mathbb{R}^d$.

Note: If $f$ is convex, with $Df$ being Lipschitz doesn't imply uniqueness of a minimum, $f(x,y)=x^2$ is an example of such function.

Proof Recall that the gradient descent update rule is given by: $$ P_{k+1} = P_k - \eta \nabla f(P_k) $$ where $ \eta $ is the step size (learning rate). We aim to show that this sequence $ P_k $ converges to the global minimum.

Since $ f $ is strongly convex, there exists a constant $ \mu > 0 $ such that for any $ P $ and $ Q $, $$ f(Q) \geq f(P) + \nabla f(P)^T (Q - P) + \frac{\mu}{2} \| P - Q \|^2. $$

Using the Lipschitz continuity of $ \nabla f $, we also have that there exists $ L > 0 $ such that: $$ \| \nabla f(P) - \nabla f(Q) \| \leq L \| P - Q \|. $$

Now, consider the difference in function values at consecutive points in the gradient descent sequence. The Lipschitz continuity of $\nabla f$ gives: $$ f(P_{k+1}) \leq f(P_k) + \nabla f(P_k)^T (P_{k+1} - P_k) + \frac{L}{2} \| P_{k+1} - P_k \|^2. $$

Substitute $ P_{k+1} = P_k - \eta \nabla f(P_k) $ into this inequality: $$ f(P_{k+1}) \leq f(P_k) - \eta \| \nabla f(P_k) \|^2 + \frac{L \eta^2}{2} \| \nabla f(P_k) \|^2. $$ This simplifies to: $$ f(P_{k+1}) \leq f(P_k) - \left( \eta - \frac{L \eta^2}{2} \right) \| \nabla f(P_k) \|^2. $$ For convergence, we require $ \eta $ to satisfy $ \eta < \frac{2}{L} $. In this case, the term $ \eta - \frac{L \eta^2}{2} $ is positive, and thus $ f(P_{k+1}) \leq f(P_k) $, implying that the function values decrease with each iteration of gradient descent.

Since $ f(P) $ is bounded below, the sequence $ f(P_k) $ converges, say to $m_f$.

The equation $$ f(P_{k+1}) \leq f(P_k) - \left( \eta - \frac{L \eta^2}{2} \right) \| \nabla f(P_k) \|^2. $$ can be rewrite as $$ 0\leq\left( \eta - \frac{L \eta^2}{2} \right) \|\nabla f(P_k) \|^2 \leq f(P_k)-f(P_{k+1}) $$

Sum up above expression from $k=0$ to $N$, we obtain

$$ 0\leq\left( \eta - \frac{L \eta^2}{2} \right) \sum_{k=0}^{N}\|\nabla f(P_k) \|^2 \leq f(P_0)-f(P_{N+1})\leq f(P_0)-m_f $$

This means the series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|\nabla f(P_k) \|^2$ is convergent, and therefore $\nabla f(P_k)\to 0$ as $k\to\infty$.

Finally, we use strong convexity. Let $P_c$ be the minimum of $f$. And this we leave it for keen readers to entertain themselves to prove that it exists and is unique. Since $P_c$ is the minimum, $\nabla f(P_c)=0$. Apply the strong convexity inequality with $(P,Q)=(P_c,P_k)$ and with $(P,Q)=(P_k,P_c)$: $$ f(P_k)\geq f(P_c)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|P_k-P_c\|^2,\qquad f(P_c)\geq f(P_k)+\nabla f(P_k)^T(P_c-P_k)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|P_k-P_c\|^2. $$ Adding the two inequalities and using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we get $$ \mu\|P_k-P_c\|^2\leq \nabla f(P_k)^T(P_k-P_c)\leq \|\nabla f(P_k)\|\,\|P_k-P_c\|. $$ Therefore $$ \|P_k-P_c\|\leq \frac{1}{\mu}\|\nabla f(P_k)\|\to 0, $$ that is, $P_k$ converges to $P_c$.

4.1 Stochastic Gradient Descent

Consider the problem of training a neural network $NN(x)$ to learn a function $F:\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}$. We don't know $F$ but we have $N$ samples of it, i.e., the data of $F$. Let $D=\{(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),...,(x_N,y_N)\}$ be the data we collect about $F$.

Let's say we fix a neural network architecture, in other word, we fix the number of layers and the number of neuron the neural network has. This specification dictates the size of parameter $P$. Let's say $P\in \mathbb{R}^K$. Then the empirical loss function \begin{align*} \text{loss}_D(P) &= \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^N |F(x_i) - NN_P(x_i)|^2}{N} \\ &= \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^N |y_i - NN_P(x_i)|^2}{N} \end{align*} This is a function from $\mathbb{R}^K\to \mathbb{R}$. Training the neural network means using the gradient descent, GD ,to update the parameter $P$. Now if $N$ is large, the loss function for the whole data set can be computationally costly and the network can overfit the data. Stochastic gradient descent is proposed to solve these issues. Instead of computing the loss function for all $N$ data points, we instead compute it on subset of size $n$ where $n$ is much smaller than $N$.

Now fix $n<N$, the stochastic gradient descent algorithm is as follow:

  1. Select random $n$ data points from $D$, this make $\tilde{D}\subset D$.
  2. Form loss function with respect to $\tilde{D}$, $\text{loss}_{\tilde{D}}(P)$.
  3. Use gradient descent to update $P$.
  4. Select new $n$ data points from $D$ randomly, call it $\tilde{D}$ again.
  5. Form $\text{loss}_{\tilde{D}}(P)$.
  6. Update $P$ using gradient descent.
  7. Repeat the above step.

The number $n$ used to compute the stochastic loss function is call batch size. Let's suppose we have $N=1000$ data points, if we make batch size $n=20$, then goes through all data points we must run the SGD $1000/20=50$ times. When we run through all the data, people say it is an epoch.

Remark: An interesting question is how do we know that evolution of $P_k$ via SGD algorithm converge to a stable position, let alone a global minimum.

5. Recursive Language Models

A sentence is a string of words and a language is a collection of sentences. We let $\Sigma$ be the set of words spoken in a language. Denote by $\Sigma^*$ the set of all finite sentences, whether they make sense or not. The language $\mathcal{L}$ is the set of sentences that are spoken or written, this means $\mathcal{L}\subset\Sigma^*$. Note that we haven't yet defined what spoken means and what counts as a word. For now we can treat $\Sigma$ as just a set, and a language as a collection of strings of symbols we find when we observe a language. In this point of view, a sentence is whatever string of symbols we find in the wild and a language is the collection of those strings. The benefit of this general and somewhat abstract approach is that it allows us to see anything as a language.

Let us look at some examples.

Example 1. First, we can think of Khmer documents as a language. Whatever has been written or spoken is part of Khmer, and $\Sigma$ is the collection of all Khmer words.

Example 2. Another natural example is English, with $\Sigma$ the collection of all English words and $\mathcal{L}$ all the sentences and paragraphs written in English. However, English can also be represented as sentences in which each word is a letter of the English alphabet. We call this English $\mathcal{L}'$, with $\Sigma'=\{A,B,\ldots,Z\}$. In terms of the meaning they contain, both $\mathcal{L}$ and $\mathcal{L}'$ contain the same information. However, sentences in each of them are quite different. For example, the sentence "I Like Cats" is different in $\mathcal{L}$ and $\mathcal{L}'$. In $\mathcal{L}$ it is the sequence of words I, Like, Cats, while in $\mathcal{L}'$ it is the sequence I, L, I, K, E, C, A, T, S.

Example 3. Another example would be a collection of grey scale images. Each pixel is represented by a number between 0 and 255. We can view an image as a sequence of pixels, and in this view, an image is a sentence.

Example 4. Another example is the language $\mathcal{L}$ with vocabulary $\Sigma=\{0,1,2,\ldots,x,x^2,\ldots,+,-,=,(,)\}$. In this language you can write things like $(1+x)(1-x)=1-x^2$ or something random such as $x^7-x$. We can take $\mathcal{L}$ to be the set of correct formulae. This is an example of a language too.

Now, what is a language model? It is something that can produce sentences in $\mathcal{L}$. A language model should be something that behaves like a human: it should consume a sentence and then speak back another sentence. Basically, it is a function $M:\Sigma^*\to\Sigma^*$. We can build this map from a simpler function that consumes one sentence and produces only one word, $m:\Sigma^*\to\Sigma$. To make $m$ produce a sentence we do as follows. Take a sentence $S_0$. The function $m$ takes $S_0$ and produces a word $w$ in $\Sigma$, i.e. $m(S_0)=w$. We glue $w$ to $S_0$ to get $S_1=S_0w$, and then feed $S_1$ to $m$ to get $w'$. We continue doing this a number of times. We can think of the whole process as $$ M(S_0)=ww'\cdots w^{(n)}=S', $$ which is a sentence too. This way of producing one word at a time is known as a recursive language model.

6. Loss Functions for Classification Task: Cross-Entropy

Setup: We have a set $D=\bigcup_{i=1}^ND_i$ as disjoint union of subsets $D_i$ . The function $F:D\to \mathbb{R}^N$ defined by $F(x)=e_i$ if $x\in D_i$, where $e_1,e_2,...,e_N$ are the standard unit vectors, allow us to classify point in $D$ based on which subset $D_i$ they are in. For each $x$, the vector value $F(x)$ can be thought as a probability distribution on $\{D_1,D_2,...,D_N\}$. To use neural network to do classification task means to find a neural network $NN:D\to \mathbb{R}^N$ that approximate $F$ on $D$. The output $NN(x)\in \mathbb{R}^N$ should be thought of as a probability distribution.

Now need a way to compare two probability distributions. This will allow us to compare how well a neural network does a classification job.

Definition

Let's $P,Q:X\to [0,1]$ be a probability distribution over $X$. The cross entropy $H(P,Q)$ is defined as \[ H(P, Q) = -\sum_{x \in X} P(x) \log(Q(x)) \]

It would be cool if the cross entropy is a metric. However, it is not, one obvious thing is that it is not symmetric, ie $H(P,Q)\neq H(Q,P)$. What make the cross entropy a good candidate as a measure how close $Q$ is to $P$ is the following inequality $ H(P,Q)\geq H(P,P) $ This means that the smaller we can make the cross entropy, the better we make $Q$ close to $P$.

Proof Note that $$ H(P, Q) = -\sum_{x \in X} P(x) \log Q(x) $$ can be rewritten as $$ H(P, Q) = -\sum_{x \in X} \left( P(x) \log \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} + P(x) \log P(x) \right) $$ which simplifies to $$ H(P, Q) = H(P, P) - \sum_{x \in X} P(x) \log \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} $$ Now we need to prove that $D_{KL}(P \| Q) = \sum_{x \in X} P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \geq 0$. Note that $D_{KL}(P \| Q)$ is called the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. It is $0$ if and only if $P = Q$.

Now, $\log: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a concave function, therefore it satisfies the Jensen's inequality: $$ \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i \log(x_i) \leq \log\left( \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i x_i \right) $$ whenever $x_i \in [0, \infty]$ and $\alpha_i \in [0, 1]$ satisfies $\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i = 1$.

Applying this to the case where $\alpha_i = P(x_i)$ and $x_i = \frac{Q(x_i)}{P(x_i)}$, we get: $$ -D_{KL}(P \| Q) \leq \log\left( \sum_{x \in X} P(x) \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \right) = \log(1) = 0 $$ Thus, $D_{KL}(P \| Q) \geq 0$.

Back to using neural networks $NN$ to approximate probability distributions. We know that the output of the neural network is a vector $NN(x)=(s_1,s_2,...,s_N)$ in $\mathbb{R}^N$. This is not any probability distribution. To deal with this problem, we pass the result of the neural network into softmax function defined as:

Definition The function $\text{SoftMax} : \mathbb{R}^n \to I^n$ is defined by $$ \text{SoftMax}(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = (p_1, \ldots, p_n) = \left( \frac{e^{x_1}}{\sum_{i=1}^n e^{x_i}}, \ldots, \frac{e^{x_n}}{\sum_{i=1}^n e^{x_i}} \right) $$

For example, take $x=(2,-1,0.5,3,-2)\in\mathbb{R}^5$. First we take $e^{x_i}$ of each coordinate, which makes every coordinate positive, then we divide by their sum, so the $p_i$ add up to 1. The largest coordinate $x_4=3$ gets the largest probability, and a negative coordinate still gets a small positive probability. You can drag the bars of $x$ to see how SoftMax$(x)$ changes.

7. Recurrent Neural Network

Imagine we have a collection of news articles $\mathcal{C}=\{A_1,A_2,...,A_K\}$ and we want to classify them into $L$={sport, politics, health}. Each news article is a string of words. To process these articles, we need a way to represent each words numerically so that we can use mathematical techniques to do the classification task.

Assume $A_i=v_1...v_L$ where $v_{k}$ are words used to write $A_i$ and $L$ is the length of $A_i$. Let $\mathcal{W}=\{w_1,w_2,...,w_N\}$ be the set of vocabulary used to compose $\{A_1,...,A_K\}$, ordered in some manner. In the parlance of natural language processing, $\mathcal{C}$ is referred to as a corpus and $\mathcal{W}$ is the dictionary.

The goal is to represent $w_i$ numerically, one naive way to do this is to use number 1 to represent $w_1$, 2 to represent $w_2$, and so on. But the natural number has ordered structure, while we can't quite see that with words. So instead we should use vectors to represent words. More precisely, we want to find an embedding $E:\mathcal{W}\to \mathbb{R}^d$ for some $d$. One way to do this is to take $d=|\mathcal{W}|=N$ and send $w_i$ to the standard unit vector $e_i\in \mathbb{R}^N$, this is known as the one hot encoding. However, there are several issue with this method. First, the embedding dimension is large, for example there are around 600000 words in English. Second, $\mathbb{R}^N$ is a metric space, we would hope that that words $w_i$ and $w_j$ that are similar in meaning should be encoded closer compare to those that are not, but every pair of different words has distance $\sqrt{2}$ in the one-hot-encoding.

Continuous word embedding deal with the issues mentioned about the one hot encoding. There is $d\ll |\mathcal{W}|$ and a map $E:\mathcal{W} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ that allow one to encoder each word as a vector where word with similar meaning stay close to one another. This is not a mathematical statement but rather an observed fact. We recommend the paper A Neural Probabilistic Language Model by Bengio et al for detail.

The classification problem mention in the beginning now can be seen as classification of sequences of various length where each terms of the sequences are in $\mathbb{R}^d$. A more general set up is having finite sequence $x_1,x_2,...,x_L$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$, find a map $f$ to the output space $Y$, which can be continuous or discrete. This is an example of time series analysis. To deal with time series, we introduce recurrent neural network.

7.1 Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)

The text $A_1=x_1,x_2,...,x_{L_{A_1}}$, where $x_i\in \mathbb{R}^d$. Any model that can classify this text should look at the whole text to do so. Let's try formulate this question of classifying text mathematically. Basically, we want a function $f$ that take input as finite sequence in $\mathbb{R}^d$ to $\mathbb{R}^3$, such that if the sequence coming from sport article, healths, and politics, $f$ would map it to $e_1$,$e_2$ and $e_3$, respectively. We can not find such a function, what we can do is to approximate it.

One way to we can do is to train a several layers neural network that take the concatenation of $x_i$, that is long vectors. However, here we run to the same problem of large dimensional space. The recurrent neural network was proposed to process sequence of any length and at the same time require relatively small number of parameters.

A recurrent neural network (RNN) processing the sequence $x_1,x_2,...,x_T$ is defined as follow $$ \begin{cases} h_t=f(x_t,h_{t-1}) \\ y_t=g(h_t) \end{cases} $$ for all $1\leq t\leq T$, with $h_t\in \mathbb{R}^H$ for some $H$ and $h_0=0$. We should think of $h_t$ as the memory of the system up to time $t$ and $y_t$ as the output. What happen is before anything happen, $h_0=0$, the system contain no information, and at time $t=1$, the system look at the first word $x_1$ and produce a memory. Next, it look at the second word $x_2$ and use previous memory $h_1$ to produce new memory $h_2$. The process repeat until the end of the sequence. In the classification task we mention earlier, we would want to use $y_T$ to make prediction of the class the news article belong to. $h_T$ obtained by the system after it looks at all words that make up of $A_1$.

We think of $y_t$ as the output of the network. And $h_t$ are known as hidden vector and $H$ is called the hidden dimension.

To truly get the name neural network, $f$ and $g$ should be composition of affine maps and non-linear activation functions. One choice is the following $$ \begin{cases} f(x,h)=\sigma(W_xx+W_hh+b_h)\\ g(h)=W_yh+b_y \end{cases} $$ Now we present a version of the universal approximation theorem of the RNN.

Theorem Let $F_1(x_1),F_2(x_1,x_2),...,F_T(x_1,..,x_T)$ be continuous functions with $x_i\in A\subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and their output are in $\mathbb{R}^m$. If $A$ is compact, then for any $\varepsilon>0$, there exist appropriate weight $W_x,W_h,W_y,b_h,b_y$ such that $$ \sup_{1\leq t\leq T}\sup_{x_i\in A}|F_t(x_1,..,x_t)-y_t|\leq \varepsilon $$ where $y_t$ is the output of the recurrent neural network with weights $W_x,W_h,W_y,b_h$ and $ b_y$.

We will not prove this theorem here.

8. RNN Based Language Model

A language model is a function that takes sequence of words as input and output a new word. This allows one to get another new word using the word just generated and the previous word and one can keep produce as many word as one wants this way. This is called auto-regressive model.

In this section, we would build a character level language model based on RNN. What we mean is we would use one of the RNN model to process a sequence and use the hidden state to produce the resulting prediction.

The strategy is as follow. First we one hot encode each character, ie if the list of characters are $\{c_1,c_2,..,c_K\}$, then $\text{one-hot}(c_j)=e_j\in \mathbb{R}^K$. Next we will project this embedding into another space $\mathbb{R}^d$ via a map $E:\mathbb{R}^K\to \mathbb{R}^d$, this process is called continuous embedding, and it can be done by multiplying $e_j$ by a matrix $E$, which is a learnable parameters. This whole process allow us to turn a sequence of character to a sequence of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$, and from here we can use an RNN to approximate $$ P(w_{n+1}|w_1,...,w_n). $$ This conditional probability is a map $P:(w_1,..,w_n)\to \mathbb{R}^K$.

An RNN would be able to take arbitrarily length sequence and make prediction. If an RNN can learn to predict the next word based on a dependency of a long sequence, then it would be best. But we can't take too long a sequence, since the longer the sequence is, the more difficult for us to approximate the gradient, hence no effective learning.

The maximum length of sequence we choose to process is called block size. Now let's say we have block_size=32, this mean we want to use 32 character to predict the next characters. Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the text corpus we used for training. This mean during training, we take a chunk of 33 character piece of $\mathcal{T}$, $w_1.....w_{32}w_{33}$, use $w_1w_2...w_{32}$ as input and $w_{33}$ as output. The RNN goes through the embedding $x_1,x_2,...,x_{32}$ of $w_1w_2...w_{32}$ to produce $h_1,h_2,...,h_{32}$, and we use $h_{32}$ to predict $w_{33}$ by map $g:\mathbb{R}^H\to \mathbb{R}^K$ defined by $g(h)=\text{SoftMax}(W_yh+b_y)$.

Now if we want our model to take any sequence of length less than 32 to predict the next word, the data should be something like this $x_1$ predict $w_2$, $x_1x_2$ predict $w_3$ and so on. And good news is we don't need to do more work for this, to compute $h_{32}$, we already compute $h_1,h_2,...$, so what should do is apply $g$ on these hidden vector. Now the input and output look like $w_1w_2....w_{32}$ and $w_2.....w_{33}$, respectively, and the loss function is $$ \dfrac{1}{32} \sum_{k=2}^{32} H(e_{w_k},g(h_{k-1})) $$ Another thing to note is that we probably want to train a batch of data at the same time, this mean we should stable text at several places at time, and each place we would take 33 characters.

Next we want to talk about continuous embedding. Earlier, we mention that we should do one hot encoding and then map it to a low dimensional space. This is unnecessary in PyTorch. Let's me explain this. Say we have the text T=hello, then dictionary for this text is {e,h,l,o}, we can label e as 1, h as 2, l as 3 and o as 4, and the text is translated to 21334. Note that the vocabulary size is 4. The object nn.Embedding(4,3) take input of shape (batch_size,sequence_length) with each entry as "integer" and produce output of shape (batch_size, sequence_length,3=embedding_dimension). So basically, as soon as we encode a text as sequence of integers, we can feed it to nn.Embedding(4,3) to get continuous embedding. Note that 4 here is the size of the vocabulary.

8.1 The n-gram Language Model

We've seen that the RNN model earlier attempts to approximate $P(w_{L+1}|w_1,...,w_L)$. Another way to get this probability distribution is to actually count it, since this is a finite system. This is known as the n-gram model. Allow me to explain the 3-gram model and then you'll understand the n-gram model. Let's say we want to predict the next word based on 3 words before it that imitates a text $\mathcal{T}$, ie $\mathcal{T}$ is the training data. First, we can ask what the possible combination of 3 words are. If the text $\mathcal{T}$ is based on 1000 words, then there are $1000^3$ possible of 3 words sentences. Next for each of the combination $w_1w_2w_3$, we count its occurrence in the text, and count what are the word that come after it and in what frequency, from here we can compute the exact probability.

Below is a short text. A window of two words slides along it, and every time it reads "the cat", we count the word that comes after. Dividing by the number of times "the cat" appears gives the probability of each next word, which is one row of the 2-gram model. The table after it has the rows for all the pairs of words in the text.

Our goal here is not to study the n-gram model itself, but to use it to help us understand better what the RNN and other language model does.

Now what are some of the problem encounter by the n-gram model? First, one main issue is that the n-gram model would give uniform distribution for any sequence of words that doesn't appear in the text. Consider creating an 2-gram model based on a text that the sequence cat loves doesn't exist, when we ask the model to predict what word come next after this sequence, it will predict anything can happen with equal probability. We don't want something like that. The RNN model takes input as sequence of vectors, and it can process any sequence, so it doesn't face the problem that n-gram model has. One way one hope the RNN give good prediction is as follow: During embedding, as the model look through the text, it learns that the embedding of the word like and love are close, and it has seen the sequence cat likes in the text, this allow us to give a non-uniform distribution for the $P(.|\text{cat loves})$ based on what it seen for cat likes. One question one can ask is does the RNN do more than a modification of the n-gram model? By modified RNN here we mean something like this: the model first regroup all possible 3 words sentences based on their similar meaning and then does the 3-gram model on that.

The hope here is that the embedding learned during the training of the RNN is a way that allows us to embed each word in $\mathbb{R}^d$, making words with similar meaning to be nearby.

9. Transformer Model

Transformer was first introduced by Vaswani et al., in 2017 in the widely cited paper "Attention Is All You Need", where the authors tested it as a neural network-based machine translation model. One of the key ingredients in the transformer model is self attention mechanism. Andrej Karpathy thinks of attention mechanism as a way for words in a sentence to communicate with each other. The following presentation is how I like to think about the transformers.

9.1 Decoder only transformer

In the above RNN-based language model, the RNN takes a sequence of words $w_1w_2...w_N$ and then turns it to sequence of vectors $x_1x_2...x_N$ via continuous word embedding, and condenses it to get the sequence of hidden vectors $h_1,h_2,...h_N$, and we use $h_N$ to predict the next word. We can think of $h_i$ as containing richer and richer information about the sequence $x_1...x_N$ as $i$ increases.

The way RNN condenses the sequence $x_1x_2...x_N$ is highly non-linear and highly non-commutative, which makes it hard to approximate its partial derivatives, leading to ineffective learning. The transformer model is somewhat less non-commutative and look linear, and in practice it turns out to be a lot easier to train.

Now say we have a sequence $x_1,x_2,...x_N$, the vector $$ v=x_1+x_2+...+x_N $$ could contain the information of a sequence $x_1x_2..x_N$, but probably we shouldn't hope that this information is sufficient to make next word prediction. One way to upgrade this and still keep things "linear" is to do $$ v=a_1x_1+a_2x_2+....+a_Nx_N $$ where $a_1,a_2,...a_N$ are some real numbers. The appropriate choice of $a_1,...,a_N$ would give us a representation of the sentence $x_1...x_N$. The transformer model suggests somewhat different from this, it suggests we should upgrade the embedding of each word $x_i$ in the sentence to incorporate the context it is in. This context enrichment process takes embedding vector $x_i\in \mathbb{R}^d$ to context-aware vector $y_i=a_1^ix_1+a_2^ix_2+....+a_N^ix_N$ of the same dimension. The coefficient $a_j^i$ should depend on both $x_i$ and $x_j$. The idea is that $a_j^i$ tells us how much $x_j$ attends to $x_i$, therefore the name attention mechanism. Furthermore, instead of combining the vectors $x_j$ themselves, the model uses another linear transformed of $x_j$ call value vectors, denoted by $v_j$. Here is the summary of how the model work roughly.

  1. Convert $x_j$ to $q_j$,$k_j$ and $v_j$, called query, key and value of $x_j$, respectively.
  2. Compute the dot product $e_j^i=(q_i,k_j)$, called scores.
  3. Turn the score vector $E^i=(e_1^i,...,e_N^j)$ into convex hull coefficients vector via softmax, i.e., $A^i=\text{SoftMax}(E^i)$, where $A^i$ is called attention.
  4. Compute the convex combination $y_i=a_1^iv_1+...+a_N^iv_N$, where $A^i=(a_1,...,a_n)$.

Now we transform $x_1,x_2,...x_N$ to $y_1,y_2,...,y_N$. We want to think of $y_i$ as an enriched version of $x_i$, based on the context sequence $x_1...x_N$.

\(x\)\(\mathbb{R}^3\)\(q\)\(\mathbb{R}^3\)\(W_Q\)\(k\)\(\mathbb{R}^3\)\(W_K\)\(v\)\(\mathbb{R}^3\)\(W_V\)

Let me explain what I think could be the motivation, people in ML community has several different way of thinking about this and here I present what I think what happen.

First, how do we convert $x_i$ to key, query and value vectors? This is done by multiplying $x_i$ by matrices $W_K,W_Q$, and $W_V$, respectively, and these matrices are learned during training. This allows us to give different represent of $x_i$ for different usages. Computing the dot product to get $e^i_j$ allow us to measure a sense of interaction/relation between $x_i$ and $x_j$ and this computation isn't the direct dot product of these two vectors but rather their other representations.

Obviously, the computation of $y_i$ are highly non-linear since it involves using the SoftMax function. However, because we only use that as a linear coefficients, this makes $y$ like a "linear combination" of $x$. Next, we note that $y_i$ is independent of the order of $x_j$, this is rather different from the case with RNN.

The process we've seen so far is called self attention (actually almost). In this note, I refer to it as attention. Next we write down equation for attention mechanism that is more efficient, and add correction term. First we make a matrix $X=[x_1,x_2...,x_N]$, and the equation for key, query, value, score and attention is as follow

  1. $ K = W_K X $, $ Q = W_Q X $, $ V = W_V X $
  2. Score matrix $E=\left(\dfrac{K^T Q}{\sqrt{d}}\right)$.
  3. Attention = $ \text{SoftMax}\left(\dfrac{K^T Q}{\sqrt{d}}\right) $
  4. Attn($X$) = $\text{SoftMax}\left(\dfrac{K^T Q}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V = \text{Attention} \times V $

The division by $\sqrt{d}$, where $d$ is the dimension of the embedding, is to keep the variance of the scores around $1$, since a dot product of two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ has variance about $d$. This way the attention neither just averages the $v_i$ nor just selects one vector.

Now consider the question Where is ITC?. The words Where, is, ITC and ? have embedding vectors $x_1,x_2,x_3$ and $x_4$ respectively. Each of these vectors doesn't know that it is in the sentence "Where is ITC?". Our attention mechanism allows us to enrich the embedding $x_4$, which is the question, to know that it is a question about the location of ITC. If we want to answer this question, we need to find the information Phnom Penh from somewhere. The sentence "Where is ITC?" doesn't contain this information. For the model to be able to handle this kind of question, there must be some sort of bank or library of information or facts that the model can retrieve from. Let $V=\{f_1,f_2,\ldots,f_n\}$ be a collection of information; we can think of the vectors $f_k$ as some facts about the world. Then the information needed for $y_4$, which is the enriched $x_4$, is $\sum_k a_kf_k$, where $a_k$ is the amount of information $f_k$ needed. How much of $f_k$ does $y_4$ need? To compute this we can take some sort of dot product of $f_k$ and $y_4$. However, $y_4$ is a question, so the dot product is large only if $f_k$ and $y_4$ are aligned, but this is not a good thing because $f_k$ is about facts and $y_4$ is a question. To resolve this issue, each $f_k$ should have its key, let's call it $g_k$. When we want to know how much $f_k$ is needed for a query $y$, we can compute $(y,g_k)$. So the information that the library can give is $\sum_k (g_k,y)f_k$. However, this formula is still not good, because here we assume that we need to add all the information $f_k$ in the library. The question "Where is ITC?" only needs a few pieces of information, so we should have a gate mechanism to say whether $y$ needs $f_k$ enough or not. The upgraded formula is $\sum_k \sigma\big((g_k,y)+b_k\big)f_k$, where $b_k$ here is the bias and $\sigma$ is the gate function discussed in previous sections.

The above sum can be rewritten as $V\sigma(Ky+b)$, where $b=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)^T$, and $$ K=\begin{bmatrix}\rule[3pt]{16pt}{0.5pt}\ g_1^T\ \rule[3pt]{16pt}{0.5pt} \\ \rule[3pt]{16pt}{0.5pt}\ g_2^T\ \rule[3pt]{16pt}{0.5pt} \\ \vdots \\ \rule[3pt]{16pt}{0.5pt}\ g_n^T\ \rule[3pt]{16pt}{0.5pt}\end{bmatrix},\qquad V=\begin{bmatrix} \vert & \vert & & \vert \\ f_1 & f_2 & \cdots & f_n \\ \vert & \vert & & \vert \end{bmatrix}. $$ The equation $V\sigma(Ky+b)$ is a one layer neural network, and people call this process feedforward, denoted by FFN. Note that FFN works the same way as attention: the query $y$ is compared with the keys $g_k$ to decide how much of each value $f_k$ to take, but the keys and values come from the facts stored in $K$ and $V$ instead of from the words in the sentence.

\(y_4\)\((g_{1},y_4)+b_{1}\)\(f_{1}\)\((g_{2},y_4)+b_{2}\)\(f_{2}\)\((g_{3},y_4)+b_{3}\)\(f_{3}\)\((g_{4},y_4)+b_{4}\)\(f_{4}\)\((g_{5},y_4)+b_{5}\)\(f_{5}\)\(\sigma\)\(\text{FFN}(y_4)\)\(=1.8\,f_1+1.1\,f_2\)

The process of enriching word embeddings, and then adding information to them, is called attention block. In general, we would compose several attention block, so we index output of each block as $X^i$ , with $X^0$ be the input. $$ \begin{cases} X^{i+1}&=\text{FFN}(\text{Attn}(X^i))+X^i\\ X^0&=X \end{cases} $$

\(\text{Attn}\)\(\text{FFN}\)\(X^i\)\(X^{i+1}\)\(X^i\)\(Y^i=\text{Attn}(X^i)\)\(\text{FFN}(Y^i)\)\(X^{i+1}=\text{FFN}(\text{Attn}(X^i))+X^i\)

To make next word prediction, we can use the last vector in the last layer $X^L$, let's call it $x_N^L$. We can do this by using linear classifier, ie use a linear projection to get logits of the prediction, $\text{logits}=Wx_N^L$, and $P(.|x_1...x_N)=\text{SoftMax}(\text{logits})$ is the probability, where here $W\in \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^d,\mathbb{R}^K)$, where $K$ is the vocabulary size.

The model describe above is called decoder only transformer. We recommend the paper "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits" by Anthropic.

Multi-Heads Attention

People like to think that continuous word vectors of a word contains most information of that word. Now a word has several informations\aspects. The transformers help us enrich information of the word-vector $x_i\in \mathbb{R}^d$ in the context of sentence $x_1....x_N$. It might be too much to ask this enrichment to do well when $x_i$ contains so much information. So maybe we should project $x_j$ down to smaller subspace $\mathbb{R}^h$, which we should hope this selects certain aspect of linguistic information, and apply attention in that subspace to enrich the context information of that aspect. This is the justification of attention head. Also we should have many of these attention heads working in parallel and by the end we should assemble it back to get full information.

Mathematically, we are seeking for $W^i_k\in \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^d,\mathbb{R}^{d_h})$ for $1\leq k \leq h$, ie we make $h$ attention heads. Apply attention to these subspace to get $H_k^i=\text{Attn}(W^i_k(X))$. As a result we produce is $H_1^i,...,H_h^i$. Next we concatenate these attention heads vertically to get H, $$ H = \begin{bmatrix} H_1^i \\ H_2^i \\ \vdots \\ H_h^i \end{bmatrix} $$ and to bring the result vectors back to the embedding dimension, we can multiply by $W_O\in \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d_h\times h},\mathbb{R}^{d})$. The result is $W_OH$, known as multi-head attention. Since multi-head attention is a common practice, we will refer to it as attention and denote it by $\text{Attn}(X)$. Multi-head attention can be seen as allowing different heads to talk to one another, since $W_OH$ is equivalent to $$ \sum_{k=1}^h W_O^k \text{Attn}(W_k^iX^i). $$

To aid training, the dynamical equation of the attention block is modified further

$$ \begin{cases} Y^i&=X^i+\text{Attn}(\text{LayNorm}(X^i))\\ X^{i+1}&=Y^i+\text{FFN}(\text{LayNorm}(Y^i))\\ X^0&=X+E \end{cases} $$

Where $E$ is a fixed matrix, could be learnable, to make the model non-commutative, non-permutation equivariant. The LayNorm here refers to normalizing each column $x_t$ of the matrix to have mean $0$ and variance $1$, followed by a learnable scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$.

9.2 Training a Transformer: Masked Attention

When we train the RNN, we used our input is of the form $x_1x_2...x_N$ and output is $x_2x_3....x_{N+1}$. The RNN process $x_1x_2....x_N$ and produce context representation $h_1h_2...h_N$, then we use linear classifier using $h_i$ as input to predict $x_{i+1}$.

Let's say we have a transformer of $L$ layers, processing input sequence $X=X^0=[x^0_1...x_N^0]$ and produce output $X^L=[x_0^L...x_N^L]$, which is an enriched version of $x_i^0$ based on the context sentence $[x_1^0...x_N^0]$. We want use $x_i^L$ playing the role of $h_i$ to predict $x_{i+1}$ as we did in the case of RNN. However, in the making of $x_i^L$, we use the whole sentence $X$, so it is a cheat if we use $x_i^L$ to predict $x_{i+1}$ when $i<L$. To avoid this situation, masked attention is introduced. Recalled that, for a fixed $i$, the attended $y_i=\alpha_1v_1+\alpha_2v_2+...+\alpha_Nv_N$. To prevent $y_i$ to not depend on $x_j$ with $j>i$, we can just require $\alpha_j=0$ if $j>i$. This can be achieved by adding a triangular matrix $T$ to the score $E$, where $T_{ij}=-\infty$ if $j>i$ and $T_{ij}=0$ if $j≤i$. Another way to do this is to turn elements above the diagonal of attention score $E$ to $-\infty$, this is the method used in the code given below.

\(E\)\(T\)\(E+T\)\(A=\text{SoftMax}(E+T)\)

When we train the model, we use \(x^L_i\) to predict \(x_{i+1}\), this forces \(x_i^L\) to be the representation of sequence \(x_1...x_i\) rather than just a contextually enriched version of the embedding \(x_i\).

The following code is copied from Andrej Karpathy's GPT2 code. You can visit his GitHub repository here. The strategy of the code is as follows:

  1. Create attention head object

1. Create attention head object

class Head(nn.Module):
    """ one head of self-attention """

    def __init__(self, head_size):                
        super().__init__()
        self.key = nn.Linear(n_embd, head_size, bias=False)
        self.query = nn.Linear(n_embd, head_size, bias=False)
        self.value = nn.Linear(n_embd, head_size, bias=False)
        # lower-triangular ones: position t looks only at positions 1, ..., t
        self.register_buffer('tril', torch.tril(torch.ones(block_size, block_size)))
        self.dropout = nn.Dropout(dropout)

    def forward(self, x):
        B,T,C = x.shape
        k = self.key(x)   # (B,T,head_size)
        q = self.query(x) # (B,T,head_size)
        # scores (B,T,T), divided by sqrt(head_size)
        wei = q @ k.transpose(-2,-1) * k.shape[-1]**-0.5
        # masked attention: scores above the diagonal become -inf
        wei = wei.masked_fill(self.tril[:T, :T] == 0, float('-inf'))
        wei = F.softmax(wei, dim=-1) # (B, T, T)
        wei = self.dropout(wei)
        v = self.value(x) # (B,T,head_size)
        # (B,T,head_size): each position is a weighted sum of the values
        out = wei @ v
        return out

\[k_t=W_Kx_t,\quad q_t=W_Qx_t,\quad v_t=W_Vx_t,\qquad A=\text{SoftMax}\Big(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{h_s}}+T\Big),\qquad y_t=\sum_{s\le t}A_{ts}\,v_s\]

self.key, self.query, self.value are $W_K,W_Q,W_V$, and $h_s$ is head_size. self.tril builds the triangular matrix $T$, with $T_{ts}=0$ if $s\le t$ and $T_{ts}=-\infty$ if $s>t$. wei is $A$ and out is $y$.

\(X\)\(W_Q\)\(W_K\)\(W_V\)\(Q\)\(K\)\(V\)\(QK^T/\sqrt{h_s}\)\(T\)\(A\)\(V\)\(y\)

2. Assemble attention heads to create Multihead Attention object

class MultiHeadAttention(nn.Module):

    def __init__(self, num_heads, head_size):
        super().__init__()
        # a list of attention heads
        self.heads = nn.ModuleList([Head(head_size) for _ in range(num_heads)])
        # W_O: brings the stacked heads back to dimension n_embd
        self.proj = nn.Linear(n_embd, n_embd)
        self.dropout = nn.Dropout(dropout)

    def forward(self, x):
        # attention in every head, then stack the heads
        out = torch.cat([h(x) for h in self.heads], dim=-1)
        out = self.dropout(self.proj(out))
        return out

\[\text{MHA}(X)=W_O\begin{bmatrix}H_1\\ \vdots\\ H_h\end{bmatrix},\qquad H_k=\text{Attn}_k(X),\qquad h\cdot h_s=d\]

torch.cat stacks the heads and self.proj is $W_O$. Here $h=4$ heads of size $h_s=31$ give $d=124$.

\(H_1\)\(H_2\)\(H_3\)\(H_4\)\(W_O\)\(b_O\)\(\text{MHA}(X)\)

3. Create Feed Forward Layer object

class FeedForward(nn.Module):

    def __init__(self, n_embd):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(n_embd, 4 * n_embd),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(4 * n_embd, n_embd),
            nn.Dropout(dropout),
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x)

\[\text{FFN}(y)=W_2\,\text{ReLu}(W_1y+b_1)+b_2,\qquad W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{4d\times d},\quad W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 4d}\]

The same map is applied to every position $t$ separately.

\(y\)\(W_1\)\(b_1\)\(\text{ReLU}(W_1y+b_1)\)\(W_2\)\(b_2\)\(\text{FFN}(y)\)

4. Create an Attention Block object by Composing Multihead Attention with Feed Forward Layer

# assemble the components into one attention block
class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_embd, n_head):
        super().__init__()
        head_size = n_embd // n_head
        self.sa = MultiHeadAttention(n_head, head_size)
        self.ffwd = FeedForward(n_embd)
        self.ln1 = nn.LayerNorm(n_embd)
        self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(n_embd)

    def forward(self, x):
        x = x + self.sa(self.ln1(x))     # residual connection
        x = x + self.ffwd(self.ln2(x))   # residual connection
        return x

\[x\ \leftarrow\ x+\text{MHA}(\text{LN}(x)),\qquad x\ \leftarrow\ x+\text{FFN}(\text{LN}(x))\]

self.ln1 and self.ln2 are the LayNorm. Adding $x$ back is the residual connection.

        self.position_embedding_table = nn.Embedding(block_size, n_embd)
        self.blocks = nn.Sequential(*[Block(n_embd, n_head) for _ in range(n_layer)])
        self.ln_f = nn.LayerNorm(n_embd)          # final layer norm
        self.lm_head = nn.Linear(n_embd, vocab_size)

    def forward(self, idx, targets=None):
        B, T = idx.shape

        # idx and targets are both (B,T) tensor of integers
        tok_emb = self.token_embedding_table(idx) # (B,T,C)
        # (T,C)
        pos_emb = self.position_embedding_table(torch.arange(T, device=device))
        x = tok_emb + pos_emb 
        x = self.blocks(x) 
        x = self.ln_f(x) 
        logits = self.lm_head(x) 

        if targets is None:
            loss = None
        else:
            B, T, C = logits.shape
            logits = logits.view(B*T, C)
            targets = targets.view(B*T)
            loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, targets)

        return logits, loss

    def generate(self, idx, max_new_tokens):
        # idx is (B, T) array of indices in the current context
        for _ in range(max_new_tokens):
            # crop idx to the last block_size tokens
            idx_cond = idx[:, -block_size:]
            # get the predictions
            logits, loss = self(idx_cond)
            # focus only on the last time step
            logits = logits[:, -1, :] # becomes (B, C)
            # apply softmax to get probabilities
            probs = F.softmax(logits, dim=-1) # (B, C)
            # sample from the distribution
            idx_next = torch.multinomial(probs, num_samples=1) # (B, 1)
            # append sampled index to the running sequence
            idx = torch.cat((idx, idx_next), dim=1) # (B, T+1)
        generated_text=decode(idx[0].tolist())
        return generated_text

9.3 Making a ChatBot: Fine Tuning

The training we shown above is known as pre-training. What it mean is that we adjust the weight of the transformer to imitate our language. At this stage, the transformer is not yet a chatbot, i.e., it is not designed to give answers to the questions we ask. Instead, it is learning general language patterns. To turn the transformer into a chatbot, another round of training is needed, it is called fine-tuning.

The idea of fine-tuning is this. Let’s say we have a pre-trained transformer $ T $ that was trained on a large text corpus and is able to produce coherent sentences. However, while it knows how to generate meaningful language, it doesn’t yet know how to respond to specific types of questions or follow conversational patterns. Now we want it to answer questions in a certain way. To achieve this, we collect a set of question-answer pairs

$$ F = \{(q_1, a_1), \dots, (q_N, a_N)\}. $$

The answers $ a_i $ might reflect a specific tone, style, or format we prefer (e.g., polite responses or concise technical explanations). The fine-tuning process involves training the transformer on this dataset $ F $, adjusting its weights to mimic the behavior reflected in the answers $ a_i $.

In addition to mimicking the answers, fine-tuning helps the transformer learn the structure of the interaction between questions and answers, refining its ability to respond in a coherent and context-aware manner. The hope is that it can generalize beyond the training examples $ q_i $ to answer new questions $ q $ that it hasn’t seen before. Fine-tuning can also involve techniques like reinforcement learning with feedback (e.g., using human preferences to further align its behavior), which helps improve its performance in more complex scenarios.

This process ensures the transformer goes from generating general language to behaving like a chatbot, giving responses that align with the desired conversational behavior.

9.4 Universal Approximation Theorems for Transformers

Obviously someone would want to prove that transformer can approximate any function and in fact such a theorem exist. Basically they prove that a certain class of transformer can approximate any sequence of continuous functions. There are also people who prove that there are certain classes of transformer that can't approximate sequences of continuous functions. Such a theorem is interesting in its own and we refer the interested reader to look for them. 10. My thought about language model and some open questions

There are lots of discussion about whether or not a language model understand the language it's processing. A quick objection to this is that we need to clarify what we mean by understand this lead to a philosophical rabbit hole. I am just ignore that and I would just going to state my view, which is there isn't anything deep about current language models, and I think the success of LLM is due to the fact that our language is not as complicated as we've thought!. I believe there are some fundamental quantities to be discovered about language. I'll explain all these as follow.

10.1 The question of understanding

Imagine we have a set $ P =\{ p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_N \}$ of distinct points in the plane. We cluster them into $ P = \bigcup_{i=1}^k P_i $, with $ k < N $, this might be considered as different type of part of speech. Equip the set $ \{P_1, P_2, \ldots, P_k\} $ with partial order $ \mathcal{R} $. Now, we define a finite sentence $ p_{s_1} \ldots p_{s_l} $ of point in $ P $ such that consecutive points belong to set $ P_i $ that respects the ordered relation $ \mathcal{R} $. This puts some structure on sentences one can produce.

Let's entertain that we create so many of these sentences and glue them to form a large text, denoted by $ \mathcal{T} $. Now we want to train a transformer to create sentences the manner we define earlier. Obviously, one probably guess that if $ N $ and $k$ is very large, and the structure of $\mathcal{R} $ is complicated, then it might be hard for the transformer to produce correct sentences. However, if $ N $ is not too large, and $ \mathcal{R} $ doesn't have too many sub-relation, then sure enough the transformer would remember $ \mathcal{T} $.

If a transformer can remember $ \mathcal{T} $, would we say that it understand the language we describe above? This question doesn't make sense since that language is not anything to be understood to start with, it is just tracing through a collection of points in plane. So essentially, what the transformer does is it remember the structure its see and it does this by mimicking what it see during gradient descent. So a better name should be machine imitation.

10.2 The question of complexity: Why it work?

As speculated in the previous section, if $\mathcal{R}$ is not too complex and $N$ is not too large, then it is reasonable to expect that we can train a transformer to remember the language generated via $\mathcal{R}$. The question is how to make all this precise and how to quantify this complexity. It would be interesting if such a quantity exists—one that can serve as a necessary and sufficient condition to prove that a language is parameterizable by a transformer. (You can read more about this topic in an abstract nonsense note I made called Complexity of Parametrisable Language ) . If such a measure is found, it might open up possibilities for exploring other architectures beyond transformers. Also I am not saying that our language is a form of ordered relation. The ordered relation $\mathcal{R}$ is used as illustration.

The human's mind is rather simple; that is what I've observed about my mind, the only mind I can observe. My thought is rather shallow and small in scope. What spoken by such a mind must be repetitive and therefore a sense of structure, which is grammar, emerges.

My belief is that during training, a language model does two things: (1) searches for good representations of words that reduce the complexity of the language, and (2) approximates the relationships between words, similar to an n-gram model. These two steps happen in parallel, as this is how gradient descent works.

Recall that the attention equation is $y_i = \sum \alpha_j v_j$. During training, the model adjusts both the embedding of $x_j$ and the coefficient $\alpha_j$. I believe that this adjustment reorganizes and regroups words in a way that makes it easier for the model to cluster sentences, thereby enabling it to perform a form of n-gram modeling. However, the model can only achieve this if the language we provide is simple enough.

Sometimes we see a language model generate good reasoning. People have been talking about emergent phenomena in language models—saying that when the model is large enough, it suddenly can do certain things. My belief is that larger models can approximate the n-gram model better. As the probabilities become closer and closer to the true probability distribution, some forms of reasoning seen in the training text emerge, along with "facts" from the training. The reason I use quotation marks is that these are solely based on the training text. Imagine a prolific author writing fiction equivalent in size to the entire text corpus used to train large language models, and we train the model on this story. The model would then give us answers solely based on the facts within that book, while remaining completely oblivious to facts from the real world.

10.3 The Question of Reasoning: What else can it do?

Can we design a language model that can perform mathematical reasoning? I think the answer depends on how complicated the reasoning is. If we can define reasoning as a form of language, and assume we can also define language complexity, this would allow us to determine whether reasoning can be modeled.

As mentioned earlier, I believe the fact that language models work well suggests that our language is not as complex as we once thought. Now, let's consider the question of persuasion. Suppose we want to design a machine that can persuade people on social media. How would we do this? Perhaps a good way to measure how much someone likes an argument is by tracking how long they engage with it and, ultimately, whether they react positively or just move past it. We could try to fine-tune a language model to generate sentences based on two inputs: (1) the idea we want to persuade people about, and (2) the person we want the model to convince. At this point, instead of minimizing loss, we would maximize a reward function, which should combine the time spent engaging with the argument and the action taken afterward. Would this approach succeed? I think the answer depends on how complex the human mind is in terms of belief formation.

The example above is gloomy, so let me offer a more positive application of language models. Imagine we want to build an AI teacher that aligns with student sentiment and encourages students to continue learning. We could apply a similar framework to the persuasion machine described earlier. In developed countries, there is a lot of skepticism around such AI teachers, but in developing countries, where many students lack access to education, such AI teachers would certainly be better than nothing.

I first got interested in LLMs when I tried to teach my little sister English. Because I don’t have much time to prepare lesson plans for her, I ask ChatGPT to turn a particular topic into a lesson, and with some modifications, we go through the lesson. It is pretty fun to study things with my sister, but there are a lot of things I think she should know, and my time is very limited. The issue my sister and I encounter is not unique to us. The lack of good teachers is a major issue. Many people pay lots of money sending their kids to private classes and still don’t get good results. The same issue happens with medical consultation/advice, agriculture, law, and much more. I personally do not believe we can build AI that can help us cure new diseases, but I believe we can make AI help us well in low-risk tasks, and many of these tasks are important in developing countries—maybe in developed countries too.
